One Sentence, Three Meanings

While rehearsing for the Lindenberg school podcast, Sam notices: the exact same sentence ('Sam fährt morgen nach Berlin.') answers completely different questions depending on which word gets the sentence stress!

How sentence stress shifts focus
Stressed word in sentenceWhat is highlighted?Matching contrast
SAM fährt morgen nach Berlin.the person (not someone else)not Lukas, but Sam
Sam fährt MORGEN nach Berlin.the timenot on Friday
Sam fährt morgen nach BERLIN.the destination (neutral focus / new info)not to Hamburg

Rule of thumb for sentence stress

New or corrected information gets the stress; already known words stay softer and faster. In a neutral sentence, main stress usually falls on the last new content word (often right before a clause-final verb).

Practice: Stressing the Right Contrast

Exercise

Nadia asks: *'Hast du das Buch **gekauft**?'* Sam wants to correct her: he only **borrowed** it. Which word should Sam stress?

  • Nein, ich habe es **AUS**geliehen. right answer

    Correct! *ausgeliehen* contrasts with *gekauft* — and in this separable verb, the prefix **AUS-** carries the main stress.

  • Nein, **ICH** habe es ausgeliehen.

    Who has the book is already known — stressing *ICH* would mean *I did, not someone else*.

  • Nein, ich **HABE** es ausgeliehen.

    The auxiliary *habe* carries no new contrast; the contrast is on the action (*ausgeliehen*).

In conversation, sentence stress moves directly onto the word that delivers the new information or correction.
